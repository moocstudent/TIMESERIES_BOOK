## History alone hits a ceiling quickly

Breaking it takes exogenous drivers — the things that move the curve without being in it. Industry has three families.

**Environmental**: temperature, humidity, pressure, irradiance. They drive chillers, cooling, drying and heating, and they are the direct source of seasonality in a load curve. Their advantage is that a public weather forecast supplies them in advance.

**Process state**: equipment status, current batch, product type, line speed, order mix. The most informative family, because these almost directly determine power draw — and the hardest to obtain, since many plants have never joined MES data to meter data.

**Plans**: the production schedule, maintenance windows, the shift calendar, delivery dates. Their special value is that they are **knowable in advance**, which makes them the strongest features at longer horizons.

## Read two columns at once: gain, and availability

The bench computes each driver's marginal gain by leave-one-out — add it, and see how much error disappears — while marking its availability risk at inference.

| Driver | Gain alone | Availability risk | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Production plan | 43.8% | 45% | Most informative, least dependable |
| Maintenance schedule | 7.3% | 25% | Planned yes, unplanned no |
| Temperature (degree days) | 2.4% | 15% | Needs a forecast, which has its own error |
| Temperature lagged 2 h | 2.0% | 15% | Captures thermal inertia |
| Shift calendar | 0.1% | 2% | Largely redundant with hour-of-day |
| Humidity | 0.1% | 15% | Carries almost nothing on this line |

Both extremes deserve attention. The production plan's gain is overwhelming, and many plants finalise the MES schedule the night before and revise it the next morning — push the bench's plan error past 30% and its own inaccuracy eats most of the gain. Humidity sits at the other end: always available, carrying almost nothing, and adding it only gives the model one more chance to overfit.

**A driver worth three points that will not exist at inference is worth zero.** And that question belongs on day one of modelling, not in the week before go-live.

## Non-linear transforms: the U-shaped response

Load responds to temperature along a U: heat when cold, chill when hot, save in between. Feeding raw degrees to a linear model gains almost nothing.

Three common transforms:

- **Degree days** (HDD/CDD): `max(0, T - base)`, splitting the U into two half-lines;
- **Binned dummies**: cut by temperature band;
- **Splines**: smoother, more parameters.

Trees can learn the binning themselves, but a CDD feature still speeds convergence noticeably, especially when data is limited. This book uses `max(0, T-24)^1.25`, the exponent coming from the super-linear response of cooling load to temperature difference.

## Lagged response: thermal inertia

A building has thermal inertia, so today's temperature drives the cooling load two hours later. The temperature feature needs lags and rolling means rather than only the current value. How much lag is an empirical question — sweep it, which is exactly what DT3's exercise asks.
