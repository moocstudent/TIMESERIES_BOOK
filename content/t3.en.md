## Fifty lines, and the highest-return code in the book

Industrial series are strongly periodic, which means several **training-free** rules are already strong predictors:

- yesterday, same slot
- last week, same slot
- the median of the same weekday and slot over the last four weeks
- the mean of the same slot over the last four days

Run them on your own data and most industrial loads land between 5% and 12% MAPE. That number tends to stop people short the first time, because it is already close to what many papers report for deep models.

## The ranking of the baselines is itself a diagnosis

At the bench's default settings, the four-week same-slot median leads at 7.9% WAPE while the four-day same-slot mean comes last at 26.3%. That gap is not random: the latter averages weekend load into weekday forecasts.

Drag weekly regularity to zero — simulating a line with random scheduling and no clear weekend shutdown — and the ranking inverts immediately: yesterday-same-slot takes 7.9% against last-week-same-slot's 14.6%.

You can use this directly as a diagnostic.

| Winning baseline | What it says about your line |
|---|---|
| Last week / four-week median | Strong weekly regularity, steady three-shift work, weekly scheduling |
| Yesterday, same slot | Strong daily regularity, weak weekly one, scheduling varies day to day |
| Moving average | Volatility dominates and periodic structure is weak — go back to DT1 |

## MASE: the only metric with a built-in pass mark

MASE is defined plainly: **your mean absolute error, divided by the seasonal-naive model's mean absolute error**.

- MASE = 1: exactly as good as doing nothing
- MASE = 0.8: twenty percent better than doing nothing
- MASE > 1: your model has negative value

It is the only metric that can be judged without domain knowledge. Any report that says "our MAPE is 6%" without the corresponding baseline number contains no information — 6% may be excellent, or it may be worse than copying yesterday.

## Keep the baseline running as a control group

This chapter's most important discipline is not computing a baseline once but **keeping it running in production**.

The reason is that the baseline moves. The data distribution shifts, the line changes its scheduling rules, the season turns — and the naive baseline's score moves with them. The two points of margin you had three months ago may be long gone, and if you only computed the baseline at the start of the project you will not know.

The practice is simple: every morning, score the model and the baseline on the same data and the same window and append both numbers to one JSONL file. Three consecutive days where the model loses is a signal a human should look at, and OP2 wires exactly that into drift monitoring.

## How to use the bar

The bar has three uses.

1. **At approval**: if the baseline already meets the business requirement, this project may not need machine learning at all. That is an unwelcome conclusion, and it prevents a great many wasted projects every year.
2. **During development**: a model's first comparison is always the current baseline, never the previous version of itself.
3. **Before go-live**: the multi-fold backtest mean must be significantly better than the baseline, where "significant" means the direction of the difference is consistent across most folds (see EV1).

## Exercises

1. Run all four baselines on your own data and record WAPE, MAPE and MASE. Which wins, and what does that say about your line?
2. Change the evaluation window from 14 days to 30. Did the ranking change? If it did, your window was too short.
3. Stand up the daily control from this chapter's script and run it for two weeks. How much does the baseline's own score move?
4. Consider: if your model beats the baseline by 1.5 points while the baseline's own week-to-week variation is 2 points, does that "better" hold? EV1 answers this head on.
