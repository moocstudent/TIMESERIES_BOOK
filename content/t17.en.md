## Something new

The last two or three years produced **foundation models** pretrained on enormous time-series corpora that forecast unseen series out of the box. Chronos quantises values into tokens and trains them like a language model; TimesFM is a decoder built for long context; Moirai targets multivariate and multi-frequency data; Time-MoE uses sparse experts to grow parameters while keeping inference cheap.

For an industrial practitioner, what they change is not final accuracy but the **order of operations**.

## The decision chain: three steps, in order

**Step one: run zero-shot.** No training, no GPU — small variants infer on CPU — and five minutes to a result.

The bench quantifies what that is worth: on new equipment with three days of history, a corpus model that has never seen your line scores 6.6% while the model you trained yourself scores 9.8%. It is borrowing the daily cycle, temperature response and shift shape of other plants.

If zero-shot already meets your accuracy requirement, the modelling part of this project is over. Write the conclusion down and go do the operations work.

**Step two: tune the inputs, not the model.** When zero-shot falls short, check three free things first.

- **Context length.** These models are extremely sensitive to it — 512 points and 2048 points give quite different answers, so always sweep it.
- **Covariate support.** Some accept exogenous variables and some do not at all, and your temperature feature may be the decisive one.
- **Frequency hint.** Whether to aggregate fifteen-minute data to hourly first sometimes matters more than expected.

**Step three, and only then, fine-tune.** This is the first action in the book that genuinely needs a GPU.

## The fine-tuning threshold, and a real failure mode

Full fine-tuning of a 200M model wants roughly 8–16 GB of VRAM; parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA fit under 6 GB. Those two numbers decide where you go looking for a card in the next module.

More important is **when not to fine-tune**. On the bench, fine-tuning with only three days of history (288 rows) comes out worse than zero-shot: your 288 rows drag a prior learned from fifty thousand by seventy percent — toward the noise in your three days.

Being able to fine-tune and being right to fine-tune are different things. This chapter's gate:

| Condition | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Gap between zero-shot and the target | More than 1.5 points to be worth it |
| Your own history | At least 90 days |
| Method | Prefer LoRA when data is thin |

## When they genuinely shine

Honestly: for a single plant with strong periodicity and good exogenous data, a well-tuned LightGBM is frequently still the best value.

Foundation models shine in two situations:

1. **Cold start**: new equipment with no history and a forecast needed tomorrow;
2. **Many series, each short**: a thousand machines with three weeks each, where per-meter training simply does not work.

Those are exactly where traditional methods are weakest, which makes them complementary rather than a replacement.

1. Run zero-shot on your data across four context lengths and compare with your seasonal-naive baseline.
2. If zero-shot is worse than your current model, by how much? Is that gap worth ninety days of waiting and a GPU?
3. Take your shortest-history machine and predict it both ways. Compare.
4. Check whether the foundation model you intend to use accepts exogenous variables. If not, what happens to your temperature feature?
