## Nobody forecasts for its own sake

Making a forecast more accurate has no value in itself. The value is in a decision that improves, and industrial forecasts typically serve four decisions whose error requirements differ so much that they sometimes conflict.

**Declaration and procurement**: commit a volume in advance. Under-declare and you pay a deviation penalty, over-declare and you pay for redundancy — a V-shaped cost with a sharp kink.

**Demand or capacity control**: only the single largest fifteen-minute window in the billing period matters. Accuracy across the other 2,879 windows saves nothing at all on the capacity charge. This decision wants peak timing and magnitude; average error is almost irrelevant to it.

**Dispatch and storage**: a charge and discharge plan depends on the **shape** of the next several hours rather than any single point. How the error is distributed along the time axis matters more than its size, and being half an hour early costs differently from being half an hour late.

**Anomaly alerting**: not a regression problem at all but a detection problem. A miss costs downtime, a false alarm costs trust, and the two differ by orders of magnitude.

## Asymmetry: low and high are not the same

@fig t2-asym

Nearly all of these decisions have asymmetric error costs. In demand control, breaking the cap costs six or seven times what shaving a little extra costs.

That has a precise consequence. Let the unit cost of underestimating be cu and of overestimating be co, with errors following some distribution. Then **the forecast with the lowest expected cost is not the unbiased one** but one lifted to the

τ = cu / (cu + co)

quantile of the error distribution. Switch the bench's downstream decision to demand control and you see τ = 0.882, an optimal margin of about 1.19 standard deviations, and an expected cost roughly half that of the unbiased forecast.

| Decision | low : high | Optimal τ | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Declaration | 3 : 1 | 0.75 | Lean slightly high |
| Demand control | 6 : 0.8 | 0.88 | Lean clearly high |
| Storage dispatch | 1.4 : 1 | 0.58 | Almost unbiased |
| Anomaly alerting | 12 : 0.6 | 0.95 | Lean strongly high |

## The cost table chooses the loss function, not the tutorial's default

That table decides how you train. If being low costs six times being high, train a τ=0.88 quantile model with pinball loss (ML3) rather than:

1. fitting an unbiased model with MSE, then
2. adding a constant margin to its output by hand.

The second approach looks equally effective early in a project, and it has one fatal weakness: the constant was chosen against the error distribution of that moment, and the standard deviation of the error moves with the season, with process changes, with data quality. The instant σ shifts, the constant is wrong and nobody notices. A quantile model does not have this problem, because it learns a position in the distribution rather than a fixed offset.

## The value ceiling of a forecast

Before deciding how much to invest, compute one number: **if the forecast were perfect, how much would be saved?**

Drag the error standard deviation to zero on the bench and the expected cost goes to zero. That difference is the value ceiling of a perfect forecast — no system you build can exceed it. Put it beside the one-off build cost and the monthly operating cost and you know whether the project is worth doing, and to what accuracy.

A common disappointment: the arithmetic says a perfect forecast is worth three thousand a month while the system costs fifty thousand. That is not bad news. That is something you should know before writing the first line of code.
