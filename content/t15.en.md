## The first lesson about deep models: when not to build one

This chapter starts with the smallest network: flatten the past 96 points into a 96-dimensional vector and feed a two-layer MLP.

That model already competes with trees, and it demonstrates three genuine advantages of neural networks here:

- **smooth non-linearity**: trees are piecewise constant and need many splits to approximate a smooth temperature response;
- **shared representations**: in the multi-output case all horizons share the hidden layers;
- **end-to-end learning of interactions**: you do not hand-build cross terms.

Three disadvantages are equally genuine: sensitivity to scaling, overfitting on small data, and stochastic training that requires averaging over seeds.

## Parameters against data: overfitting is arithmetic, not risk

The chapter's core is one chart: the same model's validation error against capacity at three data sizes.

| Training samples | Best capacity | Validation error there |
|---|---|---|
| 200 | 22 parameters | 14.4% |
| 800 | 88 parameters | 10.4% |
| 3000 | 160 parameters | 8.3% |

The optimum moves **right** with data. That regularity extrapolates into a rough but workable rule: roughly one parameter per twenty samples — and since adjacent time-series rows are highly correlated, the effective sample size is closer to the number of *days* than of rows, so discount it further.

Three years of fifteen-minute data from one plant is thirty-five thousand points and about a thousand effective independent days. That supports tens to hundreds of thousands of parameters, not millions. Training two million on it is not a risk of overfitting but arithmetic.

## LSTM and TCN: two routes

**LSTM** handles long dependencies with gates and a memory cell. Parameter-efficient and tolerant of variable length; slow to train because time steps cannot be parallelised, and still strained by very long sequences.

**TCN** uses dilated causal convolutions whose receptive field grows exponentially with depth, trains fully in parallel, and is simpler to implement — matching or beating LSTM on many benchmarks.

The receptive-field formula is worth memorising:

```
receptive field = 1 + (kernel - 1) x (d^0 + d^1 + ... + d^(L-1))
```

With kernel 3 and dilation base 2, six layers reach 127 — just covering a day (96 steps) — and covering a week (672) needs nine. That calculation sets your network depth, rather than a guess.

## Answer one question before reaching for a deep model

**Has your data saturated the tree model?**

Find out by plotting a learning curve: train on the most recent 10%, 20%, 40%, 70% and 100% of the training data and watch validation error against data size.

- Still clearly falling → what you lack is **data**, not capacity. Go and get more history, or more tags.
- Already flat → the tree model has absorbed what this data has to give, and a deep or foundation model is now a reasonable next step.

Adding capacity while the curve is still falling buys nothing but more expensive overfitting.

## Exercises

1. Plot the learning curve on your data. How much did the last 30% of it buy?
2. Using this chapter's rule, what is a sensible parameter ceiling for your data size — and how many parameters does your current model have?
3. Compute how many TCN layers it takes to cover your longest cycle.
4. Train the same deep model with five different seeds and record the standard deviation of the validation error. Is that variance larger or smaller than your gap to the tree model?
