## Why learn a model you will not ship

Few people push SARIMAX into production as the primary model today, yet this chapter earns a full read for three reasons.

**First, it forces you to face three basic concepts.** Stationarity (mean and variance not drifting), differencing (the operation that removes trend and cycle), and autocorrelation structure (AR terms look at past values, MA terms at past errors). None belongs to ARIMA alone — they are the language in which you judge whether any model has left structure unlearned.

**Second, it is still genuinely useful in specific settings**: a single series, short history, a need to explain the model to non-technical people, or a need for a trustworthy statistical reference.

**Third, and most practically, its residual diagnostics are a universal tool.**

## Stationarity: remove the cycle first

The bench's default state is the problem itself: the raw series has 0.81 autocorrelation at lag 96. It repeats daily and is not stationary, and an AR model fitted to it spends most of its capacity chasing that cycle.

Switch on the seasonal difference D — subtract the value 96 steps back — and ACF(96) collapses from 0.81 to −0.09. The cycle is gone, and what remains is what the model should be learning.

First differencing d and seasonal differencing D solve different problems: d attacks trend, D attacks cycle. Industrial load usually needs D=1 and often no d at all — over-differencing destroys the signal-to-noise ratio.

## Residual diagnostics: a check-up for any model

This is what the chapter is really for. Plot **any** model's residuals as a correlogram.

- A remaining significant spike says the model missed a cycle.
- The Ljung-Box test turns that judgement into a number: a Q statistic below its chi-square critical value is statistically white.

On the bench, after seasonal differencing and AR(2), Q is still above the critical value, signalling remaining structure; raising p absorbs part of it. If it is still not white at p=8, the remainder **is not expressible as linear autocorrelation** — regime switching driven by external events, for instance.

That is exactly why the next chapter reaches for gradient boosting, and the reason is measured rather than assumed.

## Using the residual plot on LightGBM

Concretely: take your backtest, compute `resid = y_true - y_pred`, and read the table.

| What is left in the residuals | Meaning | What to add |
|---|---|---|
| A spike at lag 4 | Intra-hour structure unlearned | Finer intraday harmonics |
| A spike at lag 96 | The daily cycle is not fully absorbed | Check calendar features, or model by period |
| A spike at lag 672 | The weekly cycle is not absorbed | Add a weekly lag or a day-of-week interaction |
| Non-zero residual mean by hour | Systematic bias in some hours | Shift boundaries are missing from the features |

This table applies to every model later in this book.

## SARIMAX's four ceilings

Being honest about where it stops:

1. **Several exogenous variables**: the parameterisation explodes and tuning becomes painful;
2. **Hundreds of series**: you fit hundreds of models;
3. **Non-linear regime switching**: a linear structure cannot express it;
4. **Long horizons**: at 96 steps the confidence interval widens past usefulness.

Industrial series usually hit all four, which is why its place is as a reference frame and a diagnostic tool rather than a workhorse.

## Exercises

1. Run an ADF test on your data, then add a seasonal difference and run it again. How far did the p-value move?
2. Fit AR(p) for p from 1 to 8 and watch the Ljung-Box Q fall. Does it drop below the critical value?
3. Plot the residual ACF of your current best model, whatever it is, and find the largest spike's lag.
4. Group the residuals by hour and take the mean. Is any period systematically high or low?
