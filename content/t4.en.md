## The CSV you received is fifth-hand

That export usually travelled sensor → transmitter → PLC/RTU → SCADA or historian → data platform → your file. Every hop can change it, and no hop tells you what it changed.

@fig t4-chain

This chapter walks the chain and marks the five most common deformations. None of them is data-engineering trivia — each decides whether your label is the physical quantity you believe it is.

## Deformation one: the nature of the quantity

Does the meter report the **average power** over these fifteen minutes, or the **instantaneous power** at the end of them?

During a spiky window those differ by twenty percent — and demand billing looks only at the interval average. If your export uses point sampling while you optimise peak prediction, you have been optimising something other than the billed quantity since day one.

Switch the bench's aggregation from interval mean to last value or interval max and the peak jumps. That jump is your label-definition error.

## Deformation two: sampling is not storage

The PLC scans at 100 ms, the historian stores every 5 s, and the export aggregates to 15 min. What you call raw has already been aggregated three times, possibly by three different rules.

The common misconception here is "I want fifteen-minute data, so the intermediate detail does not matter". It does: the rule used in between decides what the fifteen-minute number is. Downsampling by max to five seconds and then by mean to fifteen minutes gives a different curve from taking the mean throughout.

## Deformation three: historian compression

To save space, industrial historians apply a deadband — store nothing if the change is below a threshold — and write only at their own scan ticks. On replay the stored points are joined by **linear interpolation**.

On a steady signal this is nearly lossless, which is exactly why the compression was approved years ago. But it **deletes entirely** any excursion that rises and falls within two or three minutes: the whole event falls between two writes and interpolation flattens it.

At the bench's defaults, 1,440 true samples a day reduce to 151 stored points, total energy is only 1.24% out, and six of fourteen inrushes vanish, taking 1.3% off the instantaneous peak. More subtly, the fifteen-minute demand is **inflated** by 6.8% by the same interpolation — compression deletes the narrow spikes and widens the ones it keeps, so instantaneous values read low and interval means read high. Two errors in opposite directions, which no single recalibration can fix.

If you are forecasting demand, that cut lands on the one thing you needed, and no alarm will fire. You will simply find that the model "mysteriously underestimates peaks".

## Deformations four and five: timestamps, and the ambiguous zero

**Timestamps**: device, gateway and server clocks drift apart. Some systems store local time with no zone marker, others store UTC. Being an hour out when joining two systems is normal rather than exceptional, and DT3 quantifies what it costs.

**Zeros**: is this zero a real shutdown, a comms dropout, or a failed instrument? All three look identical in the same column and mean opposite things to a training set. DT2 is devoted to it.

## A table you have to fill in

| Question | Who to ask | Cost of getting it wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Interval mean or instantaneous | Instrument engineer / metering | Your label is not the billed quantity |
| PLC scan period | Controls | You cannot tell which hop lost the detail |
| Historian deadband and scan period | IT / historian admin | Peaks deleted, with no alarm |
| Who stamps the time, in which zone | Systems integrator | Exogenous correlation flattened |
| Meaning of the quality codes | Historian documentation | Hand-backfilled numbers treated as truth |

## Exercises

1. Draw the full path from sensor to CSV for your own data, marking the period and aggregation rule at each hop. How many can you not answer?
2. Ask operations for one day of one-minute (or finer) raw data and compare its peak and energy with the fifteen-minute series you normally use. How far apart are they?
3. Look up your historian's deadband and scan period. Using this chapter's arithmetic, how short an excursion gets deleted?
4. Take any day and mark every point whose quality code is not zero. What share are they — and were you treating them as truth?
