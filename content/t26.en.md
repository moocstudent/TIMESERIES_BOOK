## The publication time is not yours

Between an offline score and a live service lies a gap made of time.

What hour does the day-ahead forecast publish? You do not decide that — downstream does. If the dispatch meeting is at eight, the forecast must be in the system by half past seven.

@fig t26-clock

Work backwards: how long inference takes, how long features take, when upstream data is complete, when the weather forecast refreshes. **The slowest link decides whether you deliver on time.** In a plant that link is almost always the completeness of upstream data, and it has a long tail.

## Incomplete data is the normal case

Meter data may upload two hours behind, the MES schedule may only be finalised the previous evening, the weather forecast refreshes every six hours.

At the bench's defaults, the model misses its deadline on a quarter of days. That proportion is not a data-quality problem; it is how the chain normally behaves.

So the inference service must **handle** incompleteness rather than raising it as an exception.

## The degradation path is designed, not discovered

This is the chapter's central claim. Three paths:

| Situation | What goes out |
|---|---|
| A feature is missing | Last available value plus an is_stale flag |
| The model service times out | Fall back to the seasonal-naive baseline |
| The whole chain fails | Republish the previous forecast, marked stale |

**The right answer is never to raise an error and leave downstream empty.**

The reason is behavioural: a slightly worse forecast beats nothing by a wide margin. Nothing sends the dispatcher back to estimating by hand, and once they have gone back they do not return. A system you spent three months building can lose its user over one empty output.

Switch the fallback off on the bench and downstream receives nothing on a quarter of days. Switch it on and that quarter becomes "baseline, 7.5% error, and recorded".

## Intraday rolling: the cheapest improvement after go-live

After the day-ahead forecast publishes, re-forecast the remaining hours every hour — or every fifteen minutes — as the day's actuals arrive.

The gain comes from a simple mechanism: **the effective horizon shortens**. A day-ahead forecast must see 24 hours out, while an intraday refresh sees only a few, and the error falls accordingly.

The important part is that it **requires no retraining** — you simply deploy the short-horizon direct models from FE3. It is the highest-return accuracy improvement available after go-live.

## Archiving: start on day one

Store every forecast together with its input features, model version and generation timestamp.

That archive is the only basis for later drift analysis (OP2) and incident review (OP3), and it must **start on day one**. Otherwise, three months from now, "why was the fifteenth so badly wrong" can only be answered by guessing.

The cost is low: ninety-six forecasts a day, thirty-five thousand a year, a few hundred megabytes with the inputs included. Its entire value arrives the first time you need to review something.

4. If you are not archiving yet, start today. In a year you will be grateful.
