## The metric you measure by sets the direction you optimise in

And industry's favourite, MAPE, is the most deceptive of them.

@fig t24-metrics

## MAPE's two defects

**The denominator approaches zero.** When the true value is small — night shutdown, standby, a maintenance day — a small absolute error divides into hundreds of percent. One stopped window drags a whole month's MAPE to an absurd figure while your model may be excellent during the day shift that actually matters.

**It is asymmetric by construction.** MAPE penalises underprediction more than overprediction, because the denominator is the truth. A model chosen by MAPE therefore leans high — a lean that may happen to agree with your cost table, or may be exactly backwards, and you will not notice.

## Where each of six metrics belongs

| Metric | Strength | Weakness | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| MAE | Intuitive units | Incomparable across scales | Day-to-day monitoring of one series |
| RMSE | Heavy on large errors | Dominated by one bad point | When extreme deviation is the fear |
| MAPE | Comparable | Afraid of zeros, asymmetric | When the truth stays far from zero |
| WAPE | Total as denominator, immune to zeros | Less intuitive than MAPE | **The industrial default** |
| MASE | Carries its own pass mark | Needs a season defined | Judging whether there is value at all |
| pinball | The only correct probabilistic score | Needs a quantile model | Interval forecasts |

## The most important class: priced by consequence

This is the chapter's claim. The metric that matters is decided by **the decision the forecast serves**.

- Demand control → deviation of the estimated monthly peak, and peak-timing hit rate;
- Storage dispatch → the difference in dispatch revenue under real tariffs;
- Alerting → miss rate at a fixed false-alarm rate.

None of these is in any library's default list; each takes a dozen lines to write. And they are the numbers that decide whether the project succeeded.

## The rankings contradict each other

The bench ranks one set of predictions under six metrics, and three models take three different crowns:

- by WAPE, B wins;
- by MAPE, A wins;
- by monthly peak deviation, B wins;
- by RMSE, C wins.

A is the smooth model: attractive average error, and a systematic clipping of peaks — the daily maximum off by 4.7% on average and several quarter-hours away from where it happened. If this forecast drives demand control, **A is the worst of the three despite leading on MAPE**.

Drag the night trough to 100% and the MAPE ranking inverts entirely as its denominator approaches zero, while WAPE barely moves.

## The correct order

This is not a defect in the metrics but a reminder about order.

1. Establish which decision the forecast serves (SG2);
2. let the decision choose the metric;
3. let the metric choose the model.

Reverse it — train a model, then pick a flattering metric to report — and you will spend three months optimising a number nobody wanted.

## Exercises

1. Compute all six metrics on your backtest and check whether the rankings agree.
2. Isolate the 10% of your data with the smallest true values and compute MAPE there alone. How absurd is it?
3. Write a custom metric for your decision — a dozen lines — and add it to the daily report.
4. Look back at your last report: was that metric chosen by the decision, or was it the library's default?
