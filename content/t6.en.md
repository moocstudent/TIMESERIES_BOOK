## Three trivial things, each able to sink a project alone

This chapter covers resampling, time zones and aligning external drivers. All three look like data-handling minutiae, and each is enough to turn a well-built model into rubbish on go-live day.

## The aggregation rule comes from the target, not from convenience

Going from one minute to fifteen you may take the mean, the max, the last value or the sum. Which one depends entirely on what you are predicting.

| Target | Aggregation | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Energy | Mean or sum | Energy is power integrated over time |
| Demand | **Interval mean** | That is how the billing rule defines it |
| Equipment protection | Interval max | You care whether the peak crossed a limit |
| Cumulative counter | Last value, then difference | A counter can only take the last value |

Choose wrong and your label was never the physical quantity you thought, and every modelling hour afterwards rests on that error.

## Time zones and daylight saving: two days a year, silently wrong

Industrial data is often stored as local time with no zone marker while weather data is almost always UTC. Join them directly and your temperature feature is shifted by hours.

Daylight saving is nastier: one day a year is an hour short and another an hour long. Most code silently misaligns or produces a duplicate index on exactly those days — no exception, no alarm, just two days of terrible predictions, repeating every year.

This chapter's check script does something simple: generate a year's fifteen-minute grid, count the days that are not 96 slots and the duplicated local stamps. If you are in a zone with daylight saving and that output is not all zeros, fix the loader before training anything.

## The one alignment rule: what you have at prediction time

@fig t6-align

This is the most important rule in the chapter and the most frequently broken.

For a day-ahead forecast you may only use the **forecast** temperature for tomorrow, never the **measurement** backfilled afterwards. The latter makes your offline score beautifully unreal and exposes itself on go-live day.

The bench computes the gap: the same model trained and evaluated on measurements scores 5.9% WAPE, while serving with only forecasts gives 6.6%. Those 0.7 points of illusion appear in no offline report — only on the day you ship.

The correct construction is an as-of join: for each target timestamp, take the newest forecast run that had already been issued when you published. This chapter's Python implements it. It is more work than a plain timestamp join, and it is the only honest version.

## Thermal inertia: today's temperature drives the load two hours later

A building has thermal inertia. When the outside temperature rises, the cooling load does not follow immediately — the building's thermal mass sits in between.

So a temperature feature cannot be only the current value; it needs lags and rolling means. On the bench, moving the feature lag from 0 to 2 hours — this building's actual inertia — lifts the temperature feature's gain from 13.3% to 50.5%. Same feature, same model; the entire difference is alignment.

A timestamp offset eats the same thing: one hour out and the gain falls back to a tenth. And the model does not raise an error. It simply learns that temperature does not matter.

## An overlooked corollary

Since inference has only forecasts, **the forecast's own error is part of your error floor**. If day-ahead temperature forecasts are 1.6 °C out, that uncertainty propagates through the temperature feature into your load forecast, and no amount of model tuning removes it.

That has a practical use: once your model's error approaches "the error achievable with perfect temperature, plus the propagated forecast error", further model tuning is pointless and you should be shopping for a better weather source.

## Exercises

1. Inspect your resampling code. Which aggregation does each target use, and does it match the billing or decision rule?
2. Run this chapter's daylight-saving check. Does your zone observe it, and what do those two days look like in your data?
3. On the bench, switch off "train on measured temperature" and record the gap between offline and online. That is what you may currently be over-reporting.
4. Estimate your building's thermal inertia: sweep the temperature feature's lag from 0 to 6 hours, and the lag with the largest gain is it.
