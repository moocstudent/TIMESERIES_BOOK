## What you see is not one curve but four, stacked

Open any plant's historian and that rising and falling line is the sum of four things.

At the bottom is the **baseline**: standby draw, lighting, trace heating, auxiliary fans that never switch off. It barely moves — often less than two percent across a day. It deserves none of your model's capacity, but it decides two things: where the floor of your curve sits, and whether metrics with the true value in the denominator, such as MAPE, will explode during the night.

Above it is the **process layer**: start-ups, shutdowns, shift changes, batch transitions, production ramps. This is a square wave with the largest amplitude and the most information, and it is what you actually want to predict. At the bench's default settings it carries over eighty percent of the total variance.

Above that is the **environmental layer**: temperature driving chillers and cooling, humidity driving the dryers, a season lifting or lowering everything. It is slow, smooth, and — this is the crucial part — **obtainable in advance** from an external weather forecast. That is what makes it the backbone of your exogenous features.

On top is **noise**: measurement error, unexplainable short fluctuations, genuine randomness. In a typical industrial load it is a few percent, and the model capacity people spend the most time tuning is precisely what fits it.

## Variance share tells you where to spend your effort

Switch the four layers off one at a time on the bench and watch each one's contribution. The number has a direct engineering meaning.

| Layer | Typical share | Predictability | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline | <1% | Very high | Treat as a constant; do not model it |
| Process | 70%–85% | High, but depends on external information | The main battleground: schedule, shift, batch features |
| Environment | 10%–20% | Moderate, via weather forecasts | Non-linear transform plus lags (see FE2) |
| Noise | 1%–5% | Zero | Any capacity that fits it is overfitting |

If your process layer is only thirty percent while noise is twenty, that does not mean the line is hard to predict. It means your sampling period or your historian's compression has eaten the structure — go back to DT1 and inspect the chain rather than changing models.

## The sampling period decides what can be seen

This is the first concept that must be nailed down. A 40-second, +300 kW inrush, averaged over a fifteen-minute interval, survives as 300 × 40 / 900 = 13.3 kW, already buried under the per-sample noise.

It is not "subtle". It **does not exist in this data**. No model and no feature will recover it. Drag the bench's sampling period to one minute and the same event becomes 200 kW, visible at a glance.

So before promising anyone anomaly detection or equipment start-stop recognition, do the arithmetic: how long does the event you are hunting last, what is your sampling period, and does their ratio make the task possible at all?

## Instantaneous and cumulative are different objects

The second concept to nail down. A meter gives you two kinds of number.

- **Instantaneous** (kW, °C, m³/h): a rate. Model it directly, average it over intervals; demand billing looks at exactly its fifteen-minute mean.
- **Cumulative** (kWh, units produced, running hours): a counter that only goes up. Train on it directly and the first thing your model learns is that the number always increases — a trend that fails to extrapolate on the test set.

The correct treatment for a cumulative series is to **take the interval's last value and then difference it**, turning it back into a rate. Handle counter overflow too: a suddenly negative difference is almost always a wrap, not negative power.

## Exercises

1. Export a month of your own line's data and run one STL decomposition. Record the variance share of each layer. How much is the process?
2. Pick the event class you care most about — a machine's inrush, say — estimate its duration, and compare it with your sampling period. Does it exist in your data?
3. Open your tag list and mark every tag as instantaneous or cumulative. Is any cumulative quantity being used as if it were a rate?
4. On the bench, push noise to maximum and watch the process layer's share fall. That explains why complex models lose to naive baselines on lines whose data is dirty.
