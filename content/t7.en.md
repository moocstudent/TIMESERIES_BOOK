## If only one chapter survived

Leakage means training used information that could not exist at inference time. The consequence is not a worse model but one that **looks unreally good** — so you walk into a meeting with a false score, fund the project, commit to a KPI, and collect the real number after go-live.

With all four switches on, the bench reports 5.0% where the honest scheme gives 6.6%. That 1.6 points of inflation is a 24% understatement in relative terms, and it surfaces in the first week after shipping.

## Form one: shuffled K-fold

The most lethal and the most common. On a series, a random split lets the model use tomorrow's sample to predict today, and adjacent values are highly correlated — it is copying the answer.

An intuitive test: if the true values fifteen minutes either side of a validation row sit in the training set, then a model that learned almost nothing still scores beautifully on validation.

## Form two: future information inside features

The most insidious family, because it hides in feature-engineering code.

- **Scaling statistics computed over everything.** `scaler.fit(X)` before splitting leaks the test set's mean and standard deviation into training.
- **A rolling window that includes the current point.** `y.rolling(8).mean()` gives the mean of y[i-7..i], including y[i] itself — the feature is computed from the answer. It must be followed by `.shift(horizon)`.
- **lag_1 does not exist when forecasting 24 hours ahead.** This is the mirror image of FE1's rule that the lag floor equals the lead time.
- **Target encoding computed on everything.** A categorical's target mean must be computed inside the fold.
- **A data version revised later.** Industrial data is frequently backfilled and corrected; training sees the corrected version while inference has only the original.

## Form three: no gap between folds

If the forecast must be issued 24 hours ahead, the end of the training fold and the start of the validation fold must be 24 hours (96 slots) apart.

The reason: the last training sample's label is the true value at time t, while the first validation sample's lag features may land near t. Without a gap, boundary samples still leak.

## Form four: cross-series leakage

When modelling many meters, the value of another meter at the same timestamp may or may not be available, depending on your inference architecture. If your service calls the meters serially, the neighbouring meter's value at the same instant does not exist when the first one is predicted.

This must be **defined explicitly** rather than by default. Write it down: at inference, for timestamp t, which tags at which timestamps do I actually have?

## The pre-ship checklist

Turn these into a runnable test and wire it into CI.

1. Does any feature name carry a lag smaller than the lead time?
2. Is every rolling feature explicitly shifted?
3. Are scaling and target encoding computed inside the fold?
4. Is the gap between the training and validation folds at least the lead time?
5. How far apart are the same model's scores under shuffled and time-ordered splits? More than two points means go and find the leak.

The fifth is especially useful: it does not require you to know where the leak is, only to notice that the two splits disagree.

## Exercises

1. Run your current training script through all five checks and write down each answer.
2. Deliberately add lag_1 and run a shuffled split. Record the score. That is what "too good to be true" looks like.
3. Wire this chapter's leak_checks.py into your CI so it runs whenever the feature definitions change.
4. Ask yourself one question: the number you last reported — which split produced it?
