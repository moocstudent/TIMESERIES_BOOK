## Dispatch never wanted a number

"Load at three tomorrow afternoon is 1840 kW" cannot drive a decision, because it says nothing about how far off it might be. "Ninety percent confident it stays below 2050 kW" plugs straight into a demand-control strategy.

This chapter upgrades point forecasts to probabilistic ones, and the method is simpler than most expect: **swap MSE for pinball loss**. The same features and the same model, fitted three to nine times, once per quantile, give you a band.

## The whole mechanism is a kink

Underprediction is weighted by τ and overprediction by (1−τ).

- At τ=0.9, being low is penalised nine times as heavily as being high, so the model leans upward and what it learns is the 90th percentile;
- At τ=0.5 the weights are symmetric and it learns the median — which, incidentally, is L1 loss;
- At τ=0.1 it reverses and learns the 10th percentile.

No distributional assumption, no variance estimate. That is why it is more dependable than predicting a mean, assuming normality and computing an interval: the latter systematically draws the wrong band on right-skewed, heteroskedastic data like load.

## P90 is not a safe value

The most misread sentence in this chapter. P90 is **the value with a ten percent chance of being exceeded**.

Across a thirty-day month you should expect three exceedances. None at all means your interval is too wide — equally a calibration failure, and an expensive one: an over-wide upper bound makes a demand-control system trigger constantly and defer production for nothing.

## Coverage calibration: count, do not assume

Put the band on a backtest and count what falls inside. An ideal 80% interval should cover 80%. The bench measures 80.1%, with the upper bound exceeded 9.2% of the time against a nominal 10% — well calibrated.

Systematic deviation means bias or a wrong variance estimate. More important is to look **by segment**.

| | Low-load hours | High-load hours |
|---|---|---|
| Quantile regression, upper bound exceeded | 6.1% | 12.4% |
| Point model plus constant margin, exceeded | 1.0% | 20.2% |

Overall coverage is similar, but the constant margin is far too wide at night and badly too narrow at the peak — and the peak is the part you actually care about.

The reason is direct: the size of the error scales with load (heteroskedasticity), which a constant margin cannot express and a quantile model can.

## Two engineering details

**Quantile crossing.** Independently fitted quantiles can produce the absurdity of P90 below P50. Fix it by sorting each row after fitting, or by using an implementation that trains quantiles jointly.

**Which quantile to run.** Not a preference: SG2's cost asymmetry decides it, τ = cu/(cu+co). If being low costs four times being high, the operating quantile is 0.8.

## Exercises

1. Train P10, P50 and P90 with pinball loss and count the actual coverage on a backtest.
2. Split the test set into four load quartiles and count exceedances of the upper bound in each. Is the top quartile clearly worse?
3. Build the comparison: fit a point model with MSE, add a constant margin until overall coverage also reaches 90%, then compare by segment.
4. Compute τ from your own cost table, train that quantile, and see how many kilowatts separate it from P50.
