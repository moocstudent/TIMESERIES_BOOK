## Kaggle is not the only route

And for many industrial projects it is not even the best one. This chapter lays ten common options on one table and scores them on four axes.

@fig t21-matrix

## Four axes

**Cost**: the real hourly or monthly spend. A free tier is not zero cost — it costs you time and certainty.

**Reachability**: whether it works reliably from your network. Decisive for overseas platforms, and routinely underestimated by engineers until a run drops out in its seventh hour.

**Long-run capability**: whether twenty consecutive hours are possible. A platform with a session wall is not slightly worse for a long job — it is **infeasible**.

**Data residency**: which jurisdiction holds the data.

## Three tiers

**Free**: Kaggle (30 GPU-hours a week, a nine-hour wall), Colab (reclaimable at any moment on the free plan, steadier when paid), new-user trial credits from every cloud vendor, and several domestic platforms that hand out daily allowances and need no cross-border network.

**Hourly rental**: GPU rental platforms charge a few yuan per card-hour domestically, with comparable per-second pricing abroad. Their shared advantage is that there is **no session wall**.

**Local**: a desktop with a consumer card is the cheapest option under sustained heavy use, and the data never leaves the building.

## Which constraint is doing the work

This is the chapter's method: filter by **feasibility** first, and only then rank.

The bench's default constraints are data stays in country, twenty-six hours a month, longest single run fourteen hours, budget two hundred yuan. Under those:

- "fourteen hours in one run" removes every free platform with a session wall in one stroke;
- "data stays in country" removes every overseas option;
- the survivor at the top is domestic hourly rental, at roughly forty-seven yuan a month.

Drop the longest run to eight hours and the free tier returns immediately. Switch off the residency rule and overseas per-second rental becomes competitive again.

**You cannot hold all the constraints at once.** If nothing qualifies, that is itself useful information: one of them has to give, and which one is a business decision rather than a technical one.

## Practical advice on renting

If your job exceeds nine hours and the budget allows, renting a card for the price of a lunch beats wrestling with quotas and checkpoints. What that money buys is not compute but **not being interrupted**.

Once you have a box there is a standard routine, scripted in this chapter's third tab.

1. `rsync` the code and data up;
2. run **inside tmux**, because an ssh drop must not kill a fourteen-hour job;
3. pull results back every ten minutes — an hourly-billed machine should hold nothing you would mind losing;
4. **release it immediately** when finished. An idle GPU bills exactly like a busy one, and forgetting one overnight often costs more than the run did.

## Data residency: a day-one question

This deserves its own emphasis. Plant history is usually the customer's asset, and uploading it to an overseas platform is an action requiring written clearance in many projects.

Three compromises: upload only rescaled and de-identified curves, use synthetic data for architecture selection, or choose a platform that keeps the data in country.

The crucial part is **timing**. Ask on day one of modelling. Discovering in the final week that the data may not leave means every experiment you ran overseas has to be redone.

## Exercises

1. Put your constraints into the bench: monthly hours, longest run, budget, whether export is allowed. What are the top three?
2. Drop the longest run from fourteen hours to eight. Did the ranking change — and what does that say about what checkpointing is worth?
3. Ask your compliance team or your customer once: may this data go to an overseas platform? Write the answer into the project document.
4. If you have rented before, recall whether you ever forgot to release a box. What did it cost?
