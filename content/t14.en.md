## A thousand meters do not get a thousand models

Going from one meter to a thousand, or one machine to a workshop, the instinct is to train a thousand models. On small data that instinct is wrong.

A **global model** — one model learning all series at once — is usually more accurate in industrial settings, for three reasons:

1. short-history series borrow patterns from long-history ones;
2. shared calendar and environmental responses are learned once;
3. the model sees the sum of all series' samples, so overfitting risk falls sharply.

## First: normalisation

Meters differ by two orders of magnitude. Mix them raw and the loss is dominated by the largest machines while the small ones are treated as noise.

Switch normalisation off on the bench and the global model collapses immediately — the most common one-shot failure of the approach. Not a wrong idea; a wrong scale.

Two standard treatments:

- standardise per series (subtract mean, divide by standard deviation) or divide by the series mean, inverting after prediction;
- **also feed the series' scale in as a static feature** (log of the mean, say) so the model knows whether this is a large or small machine.

The normalisation statistics must be computed on the training segment only, or it is leakage again.

## Second: identity and static attributes

The series id can serve as a high-cardinality categorical, through native support or an embedding. But **static attributes** are worth more:

- equipment type (injection moulder / compressor / chiller / lighting)
- rated power
- workshop
- commissioning year
- product line

They let the model reason about **a series it has never seen**, which is exactly where cold-start capability comes from. A machine commissioned three days ago has no history, but the model has seen twenty moulders of the same type and knows what that class's daily cycle looks like.

## How much is borrowed: a measurable number

The bench quantifies it. Twenty-four series, a third of them new meters with six days of history, every series scored on the same final window.

| | Meters with full history | New meters, six days |
|---|---|---|
| Local models (one each) | 7.8% | 10.4% |
| Global model (one) | 7.8% | 8.7% |

With enough history the two tie — a dedicated model loses nothing when data is plentiful. The real gap is on the new meters: 1.7 points for free, borrowed from the daily cycle and temperature response of the rest of the fleet.

## When not to go global

When behaviour differs wildly between series — an injection moulder and a central chiller — forced sharing makes them interfere.

The right move then is **grouped modelling**: cluster by behaviour (intraday shape, weekend ratio, coefficient of variation) and fit one global model per cluster.

But do not rush to split. Dividing a fleet into four groups leaves each model with a quarter of the data, and that cost usually exceeds the interference it removes. The criterion is that the grouped result must beat the single global model by more than EV1's between-fold spread before it is worth doing.

## Exercises

1. Sort your fleet by magnitude. What is the ratio between largest and smallest? Could you train without normalising?
2. Collect at least three static attributes per series. Which of them actually distinguish behaviour in your setting?
3. Take your shortest-history series and predict it with both a local and a global model. Compare.
4. Cluster your series by intraday shape and decide how many groups make sense — then verify that splitting is worth it.
