## A tree model knows nothing about time

Feed a timestamp to LightGBM and it chops the training range into segments to fit, then meets an unseen timestamp in the test set and falls into the last split interval. Zero extrapolation.

To make it see time you must translate temporal structure into something it understands: lags, rollings, calendars.

@fig t8-lags

## Lags: let the correlogram choose them

Which lags to keep is not a guess. Autocorrelation (ACF) and partial autocorrelation (PACF) tell you which ones carry independent information.

On a typical industrial load, the ACF shows sharp spikes at lag 96 (a day) and lag 672 (a week) — the whole evidence for the daily and weekly cycles, with no domain knowledge required.

The two curves do different jobs. ACF decays slowly because the daily cycle keeps reintroducing correlation, which makes it good for finding seasonal lags. PACF cuts off where the direct dependence ends, which makes it good for deciding how many short lags matter.

## The lag floor: one line that explains half of all online failures

More important than selection is a hard constraint:

> **The smallest lag you may use equals your forecast lead time.**

To predict 24 hours ahead, lag_1 through lag_95 do not exist at inference — you will not have those true values yet. Training on them is the previous chapter's leakage.

Many "2% offline, 12% online" stories come down to this line. On the bench, drag the lead time from 1 to 96 and watch the strongest bars on the ACF turn red one by one: they carry the most information, and you may use none of them.

## Rolling statistics: the window must be half-open on the right

Rolling means, standard deviations and maxima carry recent level and volatility. Their trap is the window boundary.

```python
# wrong: includes the current instant, computing the feature from the answer
roll = y.rolling(8).mean()

# right: compute the window, then push the whole thing back by the lead time
roll = y.rolling(8).mean().shift(horizon)
```

Put it in the name too. Call the feature `roll_8_mean_shift96` rather than `roll_8_mean`, so any reviewer can see at a glance whether it was shifted — and so a CI check can verify it automatically.

## Calendars: cyclical encoding and real holiday tables

Calendar features look simple and carry two points.

**Cyclical encoding.** Hours and weekdays encode better as sine and cosine than as integers, because 23:45 and 00:00 should be neighbours rather than opposite ends of a number line. Adding a few harmonics — 1×, 2×, 3× the daily frequency — lets the model express finer intraday shape.

**A real calendar.** Chinese industrial settings must handle shifted holidays: a statutory holiday can fall on a Wednesday and a Sunday can be a make-up working day. Deriving working days from `dayofweek` alone is systematically wrong on a dozen days a year — and those are usually the days when load behaves most strangely. Load a real calendar table instead.

| Feature family | Typical count | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Lags | 4–8 | The skeleton, and the largest contributor |
| Rollings | 6–12 | Recent level and volatility |
| Calendar | 8–12 | Cycle shape and holidays |
| Exogenous | 3–6 | See FE2 |
