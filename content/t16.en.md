## Three representatives, and a paper that embarrassed the field

The last few years produced a wave of purpose-built forecasting architectures. Three, in order.

**N-BEATS** stacks purely feed-forward residual blocks: each predicts a part and passes the residual on, optionally constrained to basis functions that make the trend and seasonal terms interpretable. It proved no recurrence is needed and took one of the best M4 results of its time.

**PatchTST** brings in the transformer cleverly: rather than treating each timestamp as a token — too long a sequence carrying too little per point — it cuts the series into patches of sixteen or thirty-two and treats each as a token, combined with **channel independence**, where each variable passes through separately with shared weights. Those two choices cut compute and lengthen effective context.

**DLinear**: decompose into trend and seasonal, put a linear layer on each, add them. Under ten lines of core code, and it matched or beat a pile of transformers on several long-horizon benchmarks.

## Reporting the measurement honestly

This site's bench compares four models on the same data at the same budget.

| Model | Parameters | WAPE |
|---|---|---|
| Seasonal naive | 0 | 16.9% |
| Direct linear (whole window) | 424 | 15.8% |
| DLinear (decomposed) | 232 | 16.6% |
| Patch linear (patch=4) | 136 | 17.5% |

The model with the most parameters wins, decomposition and patching do not overtake it, and shortening the history to twenty days does not change that.

The result stands here unvarnished, because it *is* the chapter's point: **under two points separate the three, and all of them sit barely over a point from a seasonal naive with no parameters at all.** On this line the architecture is not where the error lives — the data, the features and the alignment are.

## DLinear's real warning

That paper does not say attention is useless. It says much of the gain reported on those benchmarks comes from **decomposition, patching and normalisation — preprocessing** — rather than from the model.

Switch instance normalisation off on the bench (subtract each input window's own mean before predicting and add it back after) and all three models get worse together. That is DLinear's underrated half: the model no longer spends capacity remembering roughly what level this stretch sits at, and only has to learn the shape.

So before any new architecture takes a seat, print this table. **An honestly tuned simple baseline is frequently already sitting there.**

## What patching is actually selling

Patch linear reaches within 1.7 points on 32% of direct linear's parameters. That is not an accuracy win, it is a **cost win**: the same accuracy, cheaper, faster to train and smaller in memory — which matters a great deal when compute is constrained, the subject of the next module.

It is also why PatchTST is the most practical point on the transformer route: patching shortens the sequence, so attention's quadratic cost stops being the bottleneck.

## How to run a fair comparison

This chapter's code gives a reusable frame with three requirements.

1. **Fixed budget**: the same wall-clock time per candidate, not "train to convergence";
2. **Several seeds**: report the mean, not the best run;
3. **Several folds**: use EV1's rolling backtest and report the between-fold spread alongside.

The third matters most: if the winner's margin is smaller than its own between-fold spread, that "better" is not a conclusion.

3. Sweep the patch size from 2 to 12 and plot parameters against accuracy. Where is the knee?
