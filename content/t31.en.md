## They look alike, and their persistence models are inverted

Colab and Kaggle are both a free cloud notebook with a GPU attached, so people assume one substitutes for the other. On the single most important point they are opposites, and that point decides how your training script must be written.

| | Kaggle | Colab |
|---|---|---|
| How the session ends | **Deterministic**: reclaimed at nine hours | **Random**: preemptible at any moment |
| What is preserved | `/kaggle/working` (20 GB) | `/content` **is wiped**; only a mounted Drive survives |
| How you resume | Publish the checkpoint as a dataset version, mount it back | Write the checkpoint to Drive, read it after reconnecting |
| Quota | Published: 30 GPU-hours a week | Unpublished; the free tier is best-effort |
| Background execution | Save & Run All keeps running with the browser closed | The free tier **needs the tab open** |

So the question changes. On Kaggle you ask how many segments the job splits into and across how many weeks — a scheduling problem. On Colab you ask how often to save on a machine that may die at any moment — an optimisation problem.

## Young's formula: the question has a closed form

Let one checkpoint cost C minutes and let the machine's mean time between failures be M minutes. Save too often and the time goes into writing; save too rarely and every reclaim throws away a long stretch. Between them sits an optimum:

```
tau* = sqrt(2 * C * M)
```

That is Young's formula (Daly later added higher-order corrections, unnecessary at our scale). The intuition is clear: the more expensive the checkpoint the less often you save, the less stable the machine the more often — and as a square root rather than linearly.

The bench simulates the whole process. At the defaults — twelve hours of compute, a 0.18 hourly reclaim probability (5.6 hours MTBF), three minutes to write a checkpoint — Young gives tau* = 45 minutes, and you can verify the optimum by hand:

| Interval | Expected wall clock | Thrown away | Efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6 min | 18.7 h | 0.1 h | 64% |
| 20 min | 14.7 h | 0.3 h | 82% |
| **45 min** | **14.0 h** | 0.7 h | **86%** |
| 120 min | 15.9 h | 2.8 h | 76% |
| 240 min | 20.0 h | 6.9 h | 60% |

Both ends of the U are equally bad, and the bottom lands exactly where the formula says. This is the one parameter preemptible compute genuinely asks you to tune.

## What writing to the wrong place costs

Flip that toggle from Drive to `/content` and the numbers become: twelve hours of compute turns into **45.8 hours** of wall clock, 28.2 of them thrown away, for an efficiency of 26%.

The cause is not that Colab is slower. It is that a preemption no longer returns you to the last checkpoint but to zero. The longer the job the more fatal this becomes: push the length past thirty hours and it is very nearly impossible, because an uninterrupted thirty-hour run is itself improbable.

This is the sentence to remember: **on Colab, a checkpoint that is not on Drive is not a checkpoint.**

## Two realities that consume most of the time

**Drive's small-file bottleneck.** Drive's FUSE mount is one to two orders of magnitude slower than local disk on many small files. A parquet directory split by day and tag holds a few thousand files, takes a quarter of an hour to read straight off Drive, and **has to be read again after every reclaim**, because `/content` was wiped.

The fix is to tar the dataset, copy the archive to local disk and unpack it there: one sequential read instead of thousands of random ones, cutting the restart cost from twelve minutes to two. Switch that toggle on in the bench and the wall-clock time drops visibly.

**Background execution is a paid feature.** On the free tier, closing the browser tab stops training. That decides how you work: Kaggle lets you submit and walk away, free Colab does not. A job you cannot babysit belongs on Kaggle (PF2) or a rented card (PF4), not here.

## When Colab is the right choice

Honestly, its niche is narrow.

- **Good for**: interactive exploration, trying an idea quickly, jobs of tens of minutes to a couple of hours, anything where you want to watch a plot while it runs.
- **Not for**: unattended long training on the free tier, projects that need a predictable schedule, and projects whose data may not leave the country.

One more practical note: reaching Colab from mainland China requires a cross-border network. Where that is not workable, PF4 lists the options that keep the data in country.
