## "Forecast the next 24 hours" means at least three things

The three implementations behave completely differently, and choosing wrong sends you optimising in the wrong direction for weeks.

**Recursive**: train a one-step model and feed its output back in, ninety-six times. One model, the simplest implementation.

**Direct multi-step**: train a separate model per lead time, one for h=1 and one for h=96. Nothing compounds; you pay in model count.

**seq2seq / multi-output**: one model emits the whole future curve, coherent by construction, but hungrier for data.

## Compounding is proportional to the input you must synthesise

This is the chapter's core, and a correction to the textbook version.

Textbooks say recursive forecasting compounds error. The bench is more precise.

- **Short-lag recursive** (lag1–lag4 only): by 24 hours every input is a previous prediction, and error rolls from 5.5% to 38%.
- **Recursive with seasonal anchors** (lag96 and lag672 as well): also one model, but those two lags are **true values** it never invents, so it climbs only from 5.5% to 7.5% — nearly matching direct multi-step (7.2%), which needed 96 models.

So the accurate statement is that **compounding is proportional to the share of the input you must synthesise, not that recursion is inherently bad**. A recursive model resting on seasonal anchors works very well on strongly periodic industrial data.

## Exposure bias

Recursion carries a subtler problem: the model only ever saw true inputs in training while consuming its own outputs at inference. That mismatch amplifies at the far end.

Two mitigations:

- **Noise injection**: add noise of the same magnitude as the forecast error to the inputs during training;
- **Scheduled sampling**: with some probability, replace a true input with the model's own prediction while training.

Both teach the model that its input is dirty.

## Anchoring: ninety-six models into six

Direct multi-step's real problem is not accuracy but engineering: ninety-six models to train, version, deploy and monitor.

This chapter's compromise is to **fit a few anchor horizons and interpolate the predictions between them**. On the bench, six anchors (1, 8, 24, 48, 72, 96) give 7.2% at 24 hours — identical to the full ninety-six — with sixteen times fewer models.

Note what is interpolated: interpolating **predictions** is steadier than interpolating coefficients, because coefficient space need not be smooth while predictions at adjacent horizons necessarily are.

| Scheme | Models | Error at 24 h | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short-lag recursive | 1 | 38% | Only at very short horizons |
| Recursive with anchors | 1 | 7.5% | Strongly periodic data, least engineering |
| Direct multi-step | 96 | 7.2% | The accuracy ceiling, most engineering |
| Anchored | 6 | 7.2% | The practical recommendation |

## Exercises

1. Implement all three on your data, plot error against horizon, and find the crossings.
2. Remove lag96 and lag672 from the recursive model, leaving only short lags, and watch the curve change.
3. Raise the anchor count from 2 to 12 and watch the 24-hour error converge. How many are enough?
4. If you use recursion today, check it: how many of its inputs are true values, and how many did it invent?
