## You buy the product, not one run

Combine the numbers from the previous three chapters and you can answer two concrete questions at approval: what will this cost, and how many days will it take?

```
total compute = families x hyperparameter sets x folds x time per run
```

Three families × forty sets × five folds = 600 runs; at fourteen minutes each that is 140 GPU-hours, about ¥308. At a parallelism of two, 8.8 days of wall clock.

@fig t22-budget

Note that **money and time are different axes**: money grows linearly with hours, while days fall with hours divided by parallelism. Adding a card halves the calendar and changes the bill not at all — two independent decisions.

## Lever one: low-fidelity screening

Run every candidate on 10% of the data with fewer folds and shorter training, eliminate the obviously bad eighty percent, and give full evaluation only to the survivors.

This works only if the **cheap ranking agrees with the expensive one**, and that is computable rather than assumed. The bench scores twenty real configurations on 10% and on 100% of the data and finds a Spearman correlation around 0.8; keeping the top twenty percent by the cheap ranking leaves a best survivor a fraction of a point behind the full search.

In other words, a tenth of the cost preserved most of the ordering.

But that number is **a property of your data**. If your rho comes out below 0.6, screening is not safe there — the cheap pass is measuring something else.

## Lever two: early kill

A third of the way into a run, if the validation metric is clearly behind the incumbent, terminate it. That is the core idea behind Hyperband and ASHA, and it removes roughly another forty percent of search cost.

In Optuna it is one line of pruner configuration; you do not implement it yourself.

## Lever three: the one that costs no compute

The third lever matters most and does not appear on the compute table: **get the data and the validation scheme right first**.

In an experiment contaminated by leakage, every hour above is wasted. You will very efficiently find the hyperparameters that perform best on the wrong metric.

That is also why this book places modules DT and EV ahead of PF.

## A table you can paste into an approval document

| Plan | GPU hours | Cost | Wall-clock days |
|---|---|---|---|
| No screening | 140 | ¥308 | 8.8 |
| With low-fidelity screening | 42 | ¥92 | 2.6 |
| Plus early kill | 23 | ¥51 | 1.4 |

Two switches take the plan from 140 hours and 8.8 days to 23 hours and 1.4 days, saving about ¥257. For an approval document that table is more persuasive than any technical description.
