## The model did not break; the world changed

Error doubles three months after go-live. Drift in industrial series has concrete causes, and most are **discrete events** rather than continuous processes: a new product type, an added line, a changed schedule, equipment replaced in an overhaul, a change of season, a tariff revision that moves the whole production plan.

On the day such an event happens, the error **steps up** rather than creeping.

## Did a level change, or a relationship?

The most important distinction in the chapter, and an easily missed one.

**A level change** does little lasting harm. An added line lifts the whole load by 28%, and your model uses lag features — lag_96 is yesterday's true value at the same slot, and it catches up within a day. The error jumps once and then falls back on its own.

**A relationship change** stays wrong. Commission a new chiller and the building's response to temperature changes, while the temperature coefficient in your model was fitted once at training time. Without retraining, it is wrong forever.

The bench simulates the second: a new chiller on day 80 steps the unretrained error from 8.2% to 12.5%, where it stays.

The diagnostic: if the error falls back by itself within a day or two, it was a level change. If it stays high, it was a relationship change and retraining is required.

## Two retraining strategies

**Calendar retraining**: weekly or monthly. Simple, predictable, no monitoring required; but after a drift you wait half a period on average, and in quiet months you burn compute and staff time for nothing.

**Triggered retraining**: two families of signal —

- **output side**: the rolling seven-day error exceeding the calm level by a factor;
- **input side**: the distance between the feature distribution and the training set, such as PSI above 0.25.

It responds fast, and it needs monitoring.

## The over-sensitive failure mode

Tune the trigger tight on the bench and the system retrains every week and improves nothing — its compute spent entirely on noise.

And retraining costs more than compute: every round needs validation, approval, a release, and an explanation to the operators about why the model changed again. **Frequent retraining consumes trust**, and trust is far more expensive than compute.

The steadiest arrangement in practice combines both: calendar retraining as a floor — monthly, say, in case the monitoring itself fails — a trigger for fast response, and a cooldown so it cannot fire twice in one week.

## A discipline to add: shadow comparison

A retrained model **does not ship directly**. It first runs in shadow against the incumbent on recent data, judged by EV1's multi-fold difference distribution, and stays off unless it is significantly better.

This discipline will frequently stop you shipping — which is exactly its value. A new model better by 0.2 points on average but unstable in direction across folds brings behavioural change and explanation cost without benefit.

## Exercises

1. List every discrete event on your line in the past year. Which of them are visible in the error curve?
2. Add PSI to your monitoring: pick the four most important features and compute the distance between the training and recent distributions daily.
3. Move the trigger threshold from 1.2 to 1.8 and count how often it would fire in a year. Which value is reasonable?
4. After your next retrain, run a week of shadow comparison before switching, and record that week's difference distribution.
