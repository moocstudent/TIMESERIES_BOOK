## A single split hands you a random number

DT4 established that shuffled K-fold is cheating on a series. This chapter shows what correct looks like.

**Rolling-origin backtesting** is simple in principle: pick a time point, train on everything before it, evaluate on a window after it, advance the point, repeat, and read the mean and variance across folds.

@fig t23-rolling

## Four parameters you must decide

**Window shape**: fixed (always the last N days) or expanding (always all history). Expanding sees more data, but if the process changed permanently the old data drags; fixed reacts to drift but may underfit. On the bench, lines with real process changes prefer fixed windows.

**Step**: how far the origin advances, which sets the number of folds.

**Gap**: leave a space equal to the forecast lead time between the end of training and the start of validation, or boundary samples still leak.

**Evaluation window**: a day or a month per fold, depending on your decision cycle. Too short and one abnormal day dominates the metric.

## Too few folds is what this chapter most wants to warn about

On the bench, the same model scores between 6.8% and 16.5% across eight folds — a spread of 9.7 points.

Do a single split and what you get is one random draw from that distribution, and people reliably report the flattering one. That is not a moral failing but a statistical one: on a quantity with that much variance, no single observation is evidence.

What supports a conclusion is the **mean together with its spread**.

## Comparing two models: read the distribution of the difference

The most practical rule in the chapter. When comparing A and B, do not just check which mean is lower — look at **how the difference is distributed across folds**.

On the bench A beats B on six of eight folds, with a mean difference of 0.31 points against a between-fold standard deviation of 0.92. That combination means **the direction flips between folds, so "A is better" is not yet a reliable conclusion**. Do not act on it, and do not write it in a report.

Conversely, if A wins eight of eight with a mean difference of 1.5 points and a standard deviation of 0.4, the conclusion holds.

A rough but workable criterion:

```
|mean difference| > 0.7 x between-fold sd   AND   wins on at least 75% of folds
```

## Why this matters more than changing models

A wrong validation scheme lets you work happily in the wrong direction for three months. The metric will appear to improve, you will report progress, you will be encouraged — and the improvement was inside the noise the whole time.

This book holds that experimental method decides project outcomes more than model selection does, and this chapter is the basis for that judgement.

## Exercises

1. Build an eight-fold rolling backtest on your data and record the mean, the spread and the range.
2. Take two models you believed were clearly different and compare them again by the distribution of the difference. Does the conclusion survive?
3. Change the window from expanding to a fixed 35 days and watch the error. Has your line had a process change?
4. Shorten the evaluation window from three days to one and see how much the between-fold variance grows.
